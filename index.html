How Tandem plans and delivers projects

Slipstream

A project is cut into releases. Each release gives the client one outcome they can test themselves. What was promised and the budget hold; how far each outcome is taken is what flexes.

Epics run across. Releases cut down through them.

An epic is something a person does with the product. A release is a slice across the epics that ends with the client testing one outcome. The first slice is thin and touches every epic, so the whole thing works end to end before any part is deep.

One plan, read at two levels

The client reads everything above the line, in plain language. The team and its coding agents work below it, where the technical detail lives.

One release, start to finish

The client is in the loop three times: they make the decisions the release depends on, they follow the same stories the team pulls, at any time, and they test the outcome themselves. The release review is the only fixed meeting.

Plan in outline far ahead, in detail close up

Every story is written at the start of the project. A story's plan of steps is written only when its release starts or the story is claimed, from the code as it is that day. Later releases will change after the client tests the earlier ones, so detailed planning for them would be wasted.

Outcomes and budget hold. Depth flexes.

The contract does not list stories. It promises outcomes, called commitments here, and gives each release a budget in days. A story that bundles a promise with its refinements cannot be prioritised, so stories are cut in two kinds: the core, which is the commitment at its simplest and always a Must, and the depth, which is separate Should or Could stories.

A release is measured against its days, not its date. When it is forecast to exceed them, depth is removed first, then the contract's contingency is drawn. Removed means removed: a story may return later, but that is never assumed.

What a story looks like

A story states the outcome first and says nothing about files or implementation. Its acceptance criteria are the contract: what is in them is required and proven, what is not in them is not required.

The story is the unit of work

A story is what is claimed, what waits on other work, what is ready and what is confirmed done. A developer claims a story, works through its plan and finishes it, so stories land one at a time and the client can test each as it does, instead of effort spreading across many half-done stories.

When a release starts, the technical PM draws its dependency graph between stories and inputs, names owners and sets the direction. Each story's plan is drafted by a coding agent from the code as it is that day, at release start or when the story is claimed. Anything that changes a story's scope or the dependencies comes back to the technical PM.

What a step looks like

A story's plan is an optional, ordered list of tasks, called steps. A step is a working note for the team, not a contract. It adds only what the story leaves out: the technical how. Outcome, scope, priority, release and dependencies are inherited from the story, and so are the acceptance criteria: a step points at the ones it proves. Steps are never claimed, blocked or pulled; whoever claimed the story ticks each off as it lands.

No dependencies on a stepDependencies run between stories and inputs, never between steps. If a story needs only part of another, split that story so the part it needs is a story of its own. Upkeep with no user outcome, such as dependency upgrades, is a story under a standing maintenance epic.

Work is pulled, not assigned

There is one dependency graph per release, drawn between stories and inputs. "B depends on A" means story B normally waits for story or input A to be done. A story's status is worked out from its dependencies and its claim, never set by hand: blocked, ready, in progress or done. Nobody has to work out what they can start: the ready set is every ready story in the release.

A dependency is information, not a lock, as in Jira, GitHub and Linear. A blocked story stays out of the ready set, but a person can still start it knowingly, for example by stacking a branch on the unmerged work it needs.

Each story has an owner: the developer who holds its design and approves its plan. Owning is not doing: anyone may claim a ready story, and others can help without claiming it. A developer's ready set lists their own stories first, then everyone else's, each by how many stories it unblocks.

What this gives upA dependency waits for a whole story, and two people cannot independently pull separate parts of one story. Both cost little, because no story is over two days.

One story, from claim to done

People steer; agents investigate and build. An agent never starts coding on its own plan and never merges. Three checkpoints belong to a person: the plan, each merge, and confirming the story done. The story's owner approves the plan, even when someone else claimed it. The story's branch and its pull requests, stacked if need be, belong to the story and are reviewed as one feature.

Decisions are written down once

A decision that is material and not obvious from the code gets a decision record (an ADR): a short, numbered file in the repository with the context, the choice, the alternatives and the consequences. Stories and steps point to the record instead of restating it. A spike that ends in a decision ends in a record.

Reference

There are no sprints, stand-ups, story points or velocity.

Principles

  • Outcomes before tasksA release is defined by something the client can test, not by a list of work.
  • Finish stories one at a timeA story is claimed, worked through and finished, so the client can test each as it lands.
  • Plain language above the lineThe client reads stories. The team and its agents work through each story's plan.
  • Outline far ahead, detail close upStories for the project; plans for the next release only.
  • Outcomes and budget hold; depth flexesWhat was promised is delivered within the days agreed. Dates are targets.
  • Acceptance criteria are the contractWhat is in them is required and proven.
  • People steer; agents investigate and buildPeople set priorities, approve plans and merge.
  • Decisions are written down onceMaterial, non-obvious choices get a decision record.

Who does what

RoleDoes
Technical PMOwns the plan above and below the line. Writes epics and stories, agrees priorities with the client, signs off criteria and estimates, draws each release's dependency graph and names owners, sets technical direction, runs the release review
Story ownerA developer named on a story, or a whole epic, who holds its design. Approves the story's plan and is the first call for changes inside it. Does not have to build it
DeveloperReviews the stories before each release starts, claims ready stories, works through each story's plan with agents, reviews, merges, writes decision records that arise mid-story
Coding agentInvestigates, drafts stories and their plans, implements, proves each criterion with real output
ClientFollows stories in the tracker, makes the decisions each release needs, tests the outcome, accepts in writing

On larger projects the client-facing half (stories, priorities, chasing client decisions, the release review) may be handed to a client lead. Criteria, estimates and the dependency graph stay with the technical PM.

In any tracker

Slipstream is defined by its concepts, not a tool. Stories live in the tracker the client reads. A story's plan lives there or in the repository, with the other as a mirror. Decision records always live in the repository.
